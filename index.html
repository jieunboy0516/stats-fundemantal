Step 1 of 9
Our one small story

We tossed a coin.
What can we learn?

We saw head, head, tail. We want to guess how often this coin produces heads. That is the whole starting point.

Hfirst toss
Hsecond toss
Tthird toss

What we know: 3 tosses, 2 heads, 1 tail.

What we do not know: the coin’s actual chance of heads.

We call that unknown chance p. It is just a shorter way to write “probability of heads.”

Make a guess about the coin

Keep this distinction: the outcomes are already observed. Our guess about the coin is what we are changing.
Does seeing 2 heads mean the true chance is exactly 2 out of 3?

No. Three tosses are a tiny sample. A fair coin can also produce head, head, tail. The observed fraction, 2 ÷ 3, is a sensible estimate, but it is not guaranteed to be the true probability.

Likelihood = how well a guess fits these outcomes

Which guess makes
our result more likely?

Imagine the coin’s chance of heads is 50%. What is the chance of getting our exact pattern: head, head, tail?

First head: probability 0.5.

Second head: probability 0.5.

Then a tail: probability 0.5.

For independent tosses, multiply the three chances: 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.125. That is a 12.5% chance of this exact pattern.

Now change the guessed probability

The two heads each use p. The tail uses 1 − p.

As a function of the guess p, this is called the likelihood. Larger values mean the guess makes our observed pattern more probable.

L(p)=p×p×(1−p)=p2(1−p)L(p)=p\times p\times(1-p)=p^2(1-p)

Read it aloud: “chance of head, times chance of head, times chance of tail.” The little 2 means p multiplied by itself.

The highest point is at p = 2 ÷ 3 ≈ 0.667. That is our maximum likelihood estimate: the guess with the largest likelihood.
Why multiply? And why are we using one exact pattern?

Independence means the outcome of one toss does not change the next toss’s chance. The probability of several independent outcomes occurring together is the product of their probabilities.

There are three orders with two heads: HHT, HTH, THH. The probability of “two heads in any order” is three times the exact-pattern probability. That factor of 3 is the same for every guess, so it does not change which guess fits best.

Likelihood is not the probability that our guess itself is true. We are asking about the probability of the observed outcomes if that guess were the model.

Log likelihood = the same ranking on another scale

A small mathematical
convenience.

We take the logarithm of likelihood because it turns multiplication into addition. It preserves which guess is best. That is all you need about logarithms for this step.

For example, 0.148 is larger than 0.125. Their natural logs are approximately −1.910 and −2.079. −1.910 is also the larger number because it is closer to zero.

Negative log-likelihood values here are ordinary. They occur because our likelihood values are between 0 and 1.

log⁡(a×b)=log⁡a+log⁡b\log(a\times b)=\log a+\log b

This property changes our three factors into three terms:

ℓ(p)=log⁡p+log⁡p+log⁡(1−p)\ell(p)=\log p+\log p+\log(1-p)
ℓ(p)=2log⁡p+log⁡(1−p)\ell(p)=2\log p+\log(1-p)

The symbol ℓ is a lowercase “ell.” It means log likelihood. It is not the number 1.

Watch the scale change, not the best guess

Likelihood and log likelihood have the same best-fitting p. Log likelihood is easier to differentiate because its terms add.
Score = the slope at your current guess

Should we increase
or decrease our guess?

Picture the log-likelihood curve as a hill. At your current position, does moving right take you uphill or downhill? The score gives that local direction.

Positive score: increasing p a little improves the fit.

Negative score: decreasing p a little improves the fit.

Zero score: the tangent is flat. For this coin example, you are at the top.

The gold line shows the local slope

A derivative is the rate at which a function changes as its input changes. Here the input is our guessed probability p.

U(p)=dℓ(p)dpU(p)=\frac{d\ell(p)}{dp}

Read it aloud: “The score U is the slope of log likelihood as we change p.”

What if I have never learned derivatives?

Use ordinary division: change in height divided by change in horizontal position. Start at p = 0.50.

As the horizontal step gets smaller, the ratio approaches 2. That limiting slope is the derivative. So “score = 2” is just a precise way to describe this local rate of change.

Derive the score slowly

  1. Start with the function we already know.

    ℓ(p)=2log⁡p+log⁡(1−p)\ell(p)=2\log p+\log(1-p)

  2. Differentiate the two-head term.

    The derivative of log p is 1 divided by p. We have two copies.

    ddp[2log⁡p]=2p\frac{d}{dp}[2\log p]=\frac2p

  3. Differentiate the one-tail term.

    As p rises, 1 − p falls. That produces a minus sign.

    ddplog⁡(1−p)=−11−p\frac{d}{dp}\log(1-p)=-\frac1{1-p}

  4. Add those two slopes.

    U(p)=2p−11−pU(p)=\frac2p-\frac1{1-p}

    At p = 0.5: 2 ÷ 0.5 − 1 ÷ 0.5 = 4 − 2 = +2. The fit improves if we increase p a little.

  5. Use the slope to predict a small change.

    A move of 0.01 at p = 0.5 gives an approximate log-likelihood increase of 2 × 0.01 = 0.02. The exact increase is about 0.0194.

    The slope is a local approximation. A large jump need not follow the same straight line.

The score answers “which way?” It does not, by itself, tell you how certain the estimate is.
Fisher information = how sensitive the model is

How clearly can the
data separate nearby guesses?

Two heads in three tosses is a weak clue. Eight heads in twelve tosses is a stronger clue, even though the head fraction is the same. More independent data make the fitted peak narrower.

Keep the head fraction at 2 out of 3

3-toss comparisonYour chosen sample size

The best fit stays at 2 ÷ 3. We subtract the peak’s log likelihood to align the curves. Tails below −12 are clipped.

A broad, flat peak says nearby guesses fit similarly. A narrow peak says a small change in p causes a larger loss of fit.

Fisher information is the average downward curvature of the log-likelihood curve over repeated datasets from the model. “Curvature” means how quickly the slope changes.

We take the average because different random samples give different curves. The curvature of just your sample is called observed information.

For a coin model with n independent tosses:

In(p)=np(1−p)I_n(p)=\frac{n}{p(1-p)}

Read it aloud: “Information equals the number of tosses divided by p times one minus p.” The n on top explains why four times the tosses gives four times the information at the same true p.

Derive that information formula, slowly

  1. Use general counts.

    Let n be the number of tosses and k the observed heads. Then n − k is the observed tails.

    ℓ(p)=klog⁡p+(n−k)log⁡(1−p)\ell(p)=k\log p+(n-k)\log(1-p)

  2. The first derivative is the score.

    U(p)=kp−n−k1−pU(p)=\frac{k}{p}-\frac{n-k}{1-p}

  3. The second derivative tells us curvature.

    ℓ′′(p)=−kp2−n−k(1−p)2\ell''(p)=-\frac{k}{p^2}-\frac{n-k}{(1-p)^2}

    The curve bends downward, so this is negative. Put a minus sign in front to measure the downward bend as a positive quantity.

  4. Average over many imagined repeat samples.

    If the true chance is p, the average head count is np and the average tail count is n(1 − p). Replace the random counts by those averages:

    In(p)=npp2+n(1−p)(1−p)2I_n(p)=\frac{np}{p^2}+\frac{n(1-p)}{(1-p)^2}

  5. Cancel one p from each fraction and combine.

    In(p)=np+n1−pI_n(p)=\frac np+\frac n{1-p}
    In(p)=n(1−p)+npp(1−p)I_n(p)=\frac{n(1-p)+np}{p(1-p)}
    In(p)=np(1−p)I_n(p)=\frac{n}{p(1-p)}

These are regular-model results for 0 < p < 1. Information is measured in the chosen parameter’s units; the more detailed lab explains what changes if you use log-odds.

The score is the slope. Information is the average strength of the bend. They answer different questions.
Precision = how much an estimate wobbles between samples

More information,
less random wobble.

For this demonstration, imagine the true chance of heads really is 2 ÷ 3. Repeat the whole experiment many times. In each experiment, estimate p by dividing heads by tosses.

How widely do those estimates vary?

Bars group possible estimates into 5-percentage-point bins. Probabilities come from the exact binomial model. The gold line is the true p.

With only 3 tosses, estimates can only be 0, 1 ÷ 3, 2 ÷ 3, or 1. They jump around a lot. Larger samples let the fraction settle closer to the true chance.

We measure this wobble using the standard error: the standard deviation of the estimate over repeated samples.

SE⁡(p^)=p(1−p)n\operatorname{SE}(\hat p)=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}

Compare this with information:

SE⁡(p^)=1In(p)\operatorname{SE}(\hat p)=\frac1{\sqrt{I_n(p)}}

Read it aloud: “Typical estimation wobble is one divided by the square root of information.” Here that relationship is exact for the sample fraction.

Four times as much independent data gives four times the information and half the standard error. It improves precision; it does not guarantee every single estimate is closer.
What does the square root mean? And why does n appear underneath?

A square root reverses squaring: the square root of 4 is 2 because 2 × 2 = 4. So multiplying n by 4 multiplies √n by 2.

For a binomial count K, the variance is np(1 − p). Dividing K by n to make a fraction divides its variance by n²:

Var⁡(K/n)=np(1−p)n2\operatorname{Var}(K/n)=\frac{np(1-p)}{n^2}
Var⁡(p^)=p(1−p)n\operatorname{Var}(\hat p)=\frac{p(1-p)}n

Standard deviation is the square root of variance. This produces the standard-error formula above.

For general estimators, the inverse-information variance is often a lower bound for unbiased estimators or a large-sample approximation. The detailed lab derives the Cramér–Rao bound. We have used a coin example where the sample fraction has exactly this variance.

Chi-square = add squared errors in a suitable scale

How big is the
total error?

An error can be positive or negative. If we simply add +1 and −1, they cancel, even though both are errors. Squaring makes both contribute positively.

First put errors into “usual noise” units. If an error is 6 and the standard deviation of its noise is 3, the standardized error is 6 ÷ 3 = 2.

Now suppose two standardized errors are +1 and −2. Squaring and adding gives 1 × 1 + (−2) × (−2) = 1 + 4 = 5.

Try changing the size or the sign of an error

Q=Z12+Z22Q=Z_1^2+Z_2^2

Read it aloud: “Total squared error equals the first error squared plus the second error squared.”

If the errors are independent standard normal variables—independent bell-curve noise with mean 0 and standard deviation 1—this sum has a chi-square distribution with 2 degrees of freedom.

Here “2 degrees of freedom” means two independent pieces of normal noise were squared and added. A bigger sum is farther from zero.

See what the chi-square distribution looks like

This exact distribution requires independent standard normal components. Arbitrary squared errors do not automatically have a chi-square distribution. Count tests use a related large-sample approximation, which we explain next.

Squaring removes the sign. Adding combines contributions. The chi-square distribution tells us which sums are ordinary and which are unusually large under the stated noise model.
A chi-square test = compare discrepancy with ordinary chance

Could a fair coin
produce this much imbalance?

Now collect a larger sample: 100 tosses. A fair coin expects 50 heads and 50 tails on average. It does not promise exactly 50 of each in every experiment.

Change the observed head count

Tails = 100 minus heads. The fair-coin expectation stays 50 for each.
Observed countsExpected: 50 each
Calculate the discrepancy, one operation at a time

  1. Subtract expected from observed.

    For 60 heads and 40 tails, the differences are 60 − 50 = +10 and 40 − 50 = −10.

  2. Square each difference.

    10 × 10 = 100. (−10) × (−10) = 100. The signs no longer cancel.

  3. Divide by the expected count.

    Each contribution is 100 ÷ 50 = 2. This scaling adjusts for count size: a given discrepancy matters more when expected counts are small.

  4. Add the contributions.

    Q=(60−50)250+(40−50)250Q=\frac{(60-50)^2}{50}+\frac{(40-50)^2}{50}
    Q=2+2=4Q=2+2=4

    In general:

    Q=∑(observed−expected)2expectedQ=\sum\frac{(\text{observed}-\text{expected})^2}{\text{expected}}

    The sum symbol means “do this for each category and add the answers.”

  5. Compare this sum with a chance model.

    Heads and tails are linked: if heads are 10 above expectation, tails must be 10 below. There is only one independent discrepancy, so the reference has 1 degree of freedom.

    For a fair coin and enough tosses, Q is approximately chi-square with 1 degree of freedom. The probability of getting Q at least 4 is about 4.55% using this approximation.

That 4.55% is an approximate p-value. It answers: “If the fair-coin model is true, how often would repeated experiments produce a discrepancy at least this large?”

A small p-value gives evidence against the model. It is not the probability that the coin is fair, and it does not prove the coin is unfair.

The chi-square reference for counts is a large-sample approximation, not an exact coin calculation. Also, the two count residuals are correlated; they are not two independent standard normal errors. The fixed-total constraint explains the one degree of freedom.

The test asks whether the observed mismatch is large compared with ordinary random variation under the model.
Put the pieces together

Each concept answers
one small question.

Likelihood

How well does this guess fit?

For our HHT pattern, multiply p × p × (1 − p). The guess with the largest result fits best.

Score

Which way should the guess move?

It is the slope of log likelihood. Positive means increase p a little; negative means decrease it.

Fisher information

How sensitive is the model to a small change?

It is the average downward curvature. More independent observations give more information and, in our example, less estimation wobble.

Chi-square statistic

How large is the scaled discrepancy?

Square appropriately scaled discrepancies and add them. Then compare with the relevant reference distribution.

These are related, but they are not the same quantity. The score can be negative. Fisher information is nonnegative. A sum of squares is also nonnegative.

You do not need to memorize every formula at once. First recognize the question each formula answers. Then the arithmetic has a purpose.

One last check: can you predict the answers?

  1. The score is positive. Which way do you move?

    Increase the guessed probability a little.

  2. You collect four times as much independent data. What happens?

    At the same true p, information multiplies by four. For the sample head fraction, standard error halves.

  3. An error changes from −2 to +2. Does its square change?

    No. Both squares are 4. The chi-square contribution cares about size, not sign.

  4. The p-value is small. Does that mean the null is probably false?

    It means the observed discrepancy is unusual under the null model. Turning that into a probability about the model itself would require a different analysis.

Start with the question, then follow the numbers, then read the symbols. The detailed lab is available when you want more distributions, simulations, and formal derivations.

Derive the score, one small step at a time

  1. Write down what the coin model predicts.

    Each head contributes a factor of pp; each tail contributes 1−p1-p. For independent tosses, multiply the factors.

    L(p)=pk(1−p)n−kL(p)=p^k(1-p)^{n-k}

    For the count K=kK=k, the binomial probability also has a factor (nk)\binom nk. It does not depend on pp, so dropping it leaves the estimate and derivatives unchanged. Likelihood is a function of the candidate parameter; it is not a probability distribution over that parameter.

  2. Take logs so multiplication becomes addition.

    ℓ(p)=log⁡L(p)=klog⁡p+(n−k)log⁡(1−p)\begin{aligned}\ell(p)&=\log L(p)\\&=k\log p+(n-k)\log(1-p)\end{aligned}

    Log is increasing, so maximizing the log likelihood finds the same best fit. On the plot we subtract ℓ(p^)\ell(\hat p) to put the peak at zero. This constant shift does not change the score.

  3. Differentiate with respect to the candidate.

    U(p)=dℓdp=kp−n−k1−p=k−npp(1−p)U(p)=\frac{d\ell}{dp}=\frac{k}{p}-\frac{n-k}{1-p}=\frac{k-np}{p(1-p)}

    The numerator is observed heads minus model-predicted heads. The denominator is positive for 0<p<10<p<1. Too many heads for the current model? The score is positive and asks you to increase pp.

  4. Understand a derivative as a small-change prediction.

    ℓ(p+Δp)−ℓ(p)≈U(p) Δp\ell(p+\Delta p)-\ell(p)\approx U(p)\,\Delta p

    The gold tangent shows this prediction. It is reliable for sufficiently small moves; farther away, the curve bends. A zero score means a flat tangent, not that the likelihood itself is zero.

  5. Find the top, then check the curvature.

    U(p)=0 ⟹ k=np ⟹ p^=knU(p)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ k=np\ \Longrightarrow\ \hat p=\frac{k}{n}
    ℓ′′(p)=−kp2−n−k(1−p)2<0\ell''(p)=-\frac{k}{p^2}-\frac{n-k}{(1-p)^2}<0

    With both outcomes observed, the curve bends downward everywhere and this stationary point is its unique maximum. With all heads or all tails, the maximum is at a boundary instead; an interior zero-score solution is not required.

A concrete slope

For 12 heads in 20 tosses, at p=0.35p=0.35 the model predicts 7 heads. Thus U(0.35)=5/0.2275≈21.98U(0.35)=5/0.2275\approx21.98. Increasing pp by 0.01 predicts a log-likelihood gain of about 0.220. The exact gain is about 0.214. At p^=0.60\hat p=0.60, the score is zero.

Try this: why does repeating the data change confidence?

Move to p=0.35p=0.35, then double the data. The best-fit fraction stays 0.60, but the log-likelihood gap and score double. The candidate’s relative likelihood is squared. Repeated independent evidence makes the same mismatch more persuasive.

Derive Fisher information from the score

  1. Let the data vary, while the true parameter stays fixed.

    K∼Binomial⁡(n,p)U(p)=K−npp(1−p)\begin{aligned}K&\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)\\U(p)&=\frac{K-np}{p(1-p)}\end{aligned}

    The score you saw in the first lesson used one observed count. Here KK is random because we are considering all possible repeated datasets.

  2. The score balances to zero on average.

    Ep[U(p)]=Ep[K]−npp(1−p)=0\mathbb E_p[U(p)]=\frac{\mathbb E_p[K]-np}{p(1-p)}=0

    Individual samples can nudge the model either way. At the true parameter there is no average directional push. This does not say the observed score must be zero, or that its expectation is zero at every wrong candidate.

  3. Measure the size of the random nudges.

    In(p)=Ep[U(p)2]=Var⁡p(U(p))I_n(p)=\mathbb E_p[U(p)^2]=\operatorname{Var}_p(U(p))
    In(p)=Var⁡(K)p2(1−p)2=np(1−p)p2(1−p)2=np(1−p)\begin{aligned}I_n(p)&=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(K)}{p^2(1-p)^2}\\&=\frac{np(1-p)}{p^2(1-p)^2}\\&=\frac{n}{p(1-p)}\end{aligned}

    A sensitive model has larger score fluctuations at the true parameter. Those larger fluctuations come with stronger average curvature, so the parameter estimate fluctuates less; its variance scale is the inverse information.

  4. Get the same answer from average curvature.

    Jn(p)=−ℓ′′(p)=Kp2+n−K(1−p)2J_n(p)=-\ell''(p)=\frac{K}{p^2}+\frac{n-K}{(1-p)^2}
    Ep[Jn(p)]=npp2+n(1−p)(1−p)2=np(1−p)=In(p)\begin{aligned}\mathbb E_p[J_n(p)]&=\frac{np}{p^2}+\frac{n(1-p)}{(1-p)^2}\\&=\frac{n}{p(1-p)}\\&=I_n(p)\end{aligned}

    Observed information uses your actual sample. Expected information averages over samples from the model. They can differ on a particular dataset.

20 tosses, true p = 0.35

Expected information is 20/(0.35⋅0.65)≈87.9120/(0.35\cdot0.65)\approx87.91. If the drawn dataset has 9 heads, observed information at the same parameter is 9/0.352+11/0.652≈99.509/0.35^2+11/0.65^2\approx99.50. Drawing again changes observed information; it leaves expected information fixed.

Why do variance and curvature agree in general?

Let fθ(x)f_\theta(x) be a smooth density or probability mass function. Assume its support does not change with θ\theta, and that differentiating under the integral or sum is valid.

Eθ[U]=∫∂θfθ(x)fθ(x)fθ(x) dx=∂θ∫fθ(x) dx=∂θ1=0\begin{aligned}\mathbb E_\theta[U]&=\int \frac{\partial_\theta f_\theta(x)}{f_\theta(x)}f_\theta(x)\,dx\\&=\partial_\theta\int f_\theta(x)\,dx\\&=\partial_\theta 1=0\end{aligned}

Differentiate this zero expectation again. There are two derivatives: one of the score, and one of the density weighting it.

0=∂θ∫Uθ(x)fθ(x) dx=Eθ[ℓ′′(θ)]+Eθ[U2]0=\partial_\theta\int U_\theta(x)f_\theta(x)\,dx=\mathbb E_\theta[\ell''(\theta)]+\mathbb E_\theta[U^2]
I(θ)=Eθ[U2]=−Eθ[ℓ′′(θ)]I(\theta)=\mathbb E_\theta[U^2]=-\mathbb E_\theta[\ell''(\theta)]

For discrete data, use a sum in place of the integral. Parameter-dependent support, such as a uniform distribution on a changing interval, can invalidate this argument.

Why is Bernoulli information larger near 0 and 1?

The parameter here is the absolute probability pp. The standard deviation of the sample fraction is p(1−p)/n\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}, which is smaller near the ends. Estimating a rare probability accurately relative to its own size is a different question.

Information depends on the parameter’s units. For log-odds η=log⁡[p/(1−p)]\eta=\log[p/(1-p)], the chain rule changes the answer:

In(η)=In(p)(dpdη)2=np(1−p)[p(1−p)]2=np(1−p)\begin{aligned}I_n(\eta)&=I_n(p)\left(\frac{dp}{d\eta}\right)^2\\&=\frac{n}{p(1-p)}[p(1-p)]^2\\&=np(1-p)\end{aligned}

The log-odds information is largest at 0.5. This is the same experiment in different coordinates, not a contradiction. Near a boundary, normal approximations can also be poor when successes or failures are rare.

Information turns into a precision scale when we take its inverse.

Derive information and the standard error

  1. Begin with the normal model.

    Xi∼iidN(μ,σ2),σ knownX_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(\mu,\sigma^2),\qquad \sigma\text{ known}
    ℓ(μ)=C−12σ2∑i=1n(xi−μ)2\ell(\mu)=C-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu)^2

    The log likelihood penalizes squared distances from the candidate mean. CC collects terms that do not depend on μ\mu.

  2. Differentiate to find the score and best fit.

    U(μ)=1σ2∑i=1n(xi−μ)=n(xˉ−μ)σ2μ^=xˉ\begin{aligned}U(\mu)&=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu)\\&=\frac{n(\bar x-\mu)}{\sigma^2}\\\hat\mu&=\bar x\end{aligned}

    A candidate below the sample mean has a positive score; a candidate above it has a negative score.

  3. Differentiate again to find information.

    ℓ′′(μ)=−nσ2,Jn(μ)=In(μ)=nσ2\ell''(\mu)=-\frac{n}{\sigma^2},\qquad J_n(\mu)=I_n(\mu)=\frac{n}{\sigma^2}

    Here curvature does not depend on the realized data, so observed and expected information are exactly the same. More measurements increase information. Noisier measurements reduce it.

  4. Calculate the variance of the average directly.

    Var⁡(Xˉ)=Var⁡(1n∑Xi)=nσ2n2=σ2n=1In(μ)\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}(\bar X)&=\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac1n\sum X_i\right)\\&=\frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2}\\&=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}=\frac{1}{I_n(\mu)}\end{aligned}
    SE⁡(Xˉ)=σn\operatorname{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}

    Independent variances add; dividing a random quantity by nn divides its variance by n2n^2. Standard deviation is the square root of variance, so precision improves with the square root of sample size.

  5. Standardize the error to build a confidence interval.

    Xˉ−μσ/n∼N(0,1)\frac{\bar X-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0,1)
    xˉ±1.96σn\bar x\pm1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}

    About 95% of intervals made this way contain the fixed true mean over repeated samples. In the plot, the true mean does not move; the intervals do. An individual fixed interval either contains the true mean or it does not.

    The normal sampling distribution is exact here, and 1.96 rounds the 97.5th normal percentile. If noise variance is estimated rather than known, a normal sample uses a Student t interval instead.

How much data buys a narrower interval?

At n=25n=25 and σ=2\sigma=2, information is 6.25, standard error is 0.40, and the 95% half-width is 0.784. At n=100n=100, information is 25, standard error is 0.20, and the half-width is 0.392.

Derive the Cramér–Rao lower bound

This bound says that, under regularity conditions, an unbiased estimator cannot have variance below the inverse information. Here is why.

  1. Unbiasedness responds at rate one.

    For an estimator T(X)T(X) that does not itself depend on the unknown parameter, suppose Eθ[T]=θ\mathbb E_\theta[T]=\theta. Differentiate this identity under the integral or sum:

    1=ddθEθ[T]=Eθ[TU(θ)]1=\frac{d}{d\theta}\mathbb E_\theta[T]=\mathbb E_\theta[TU(\theta)]

  2. Recognize a covariance.

    Since the mean score is zero,

    Cov⁡θ(T,U)=Eθ[TU]−Eθ[T]Eθ[U]=1\operatorname{Cov}_\theta(T,U)=\mathbb E_\theta[TU]-\mathbb E_\theta[T]\mathbb E_\theta[U]=1

  3. Use Cauchy–Schwarz.

    Covariance squared cannot exceed the product of the two variances:

    1≤Var⁡θ(T)Var⁡θ(U)=Var⁡θ(T)In(θ)1\leq\operatorname{Var}_\theta(T)\operatorname{Var}_\theta(U)=\operatorname{Var}_\theta(T)I_n(\theta)
    Var⁡θ(T)≥1In(θ)\boxed{\operatorname{Var}_\theta(T)\geq\frac{1}{I_n(\theta)}}

The normal sample mean reaches this bound, so it is efficient among unbiased estimators in this model. Biased estimators are not covered by this simple form; variance alone is also not the same as mean squared error.

Predict before moving: what if σ doubles?

Information is divided by four, standard error doubles, and confidence-interval width doubles. To recover the original standard error, you need four times as many independent measurements.

Derive the chi-square distribution and its moments

  1. Define the random quantity.

    Z1,…,Zν∼iidN(0,1)Q=∑j=1νZj2∼χν2\begin{aligned}Z_1,\ldots,Z_\nu&\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1)\\Q&=\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}Z_j^2\sim\chi^2_\nu\end{aligned}

    A value of −2 and a value of +2 each contribute 4. This makes QQ a squared distance, not a signed average. The components must be independent standard normals for this exact result.

  2. Derive one square by changing variables.

    For Y=Z2Y=Z^2, a positive yy has two possible original values, z=yz=\sqrt y and z=−yz=-\sqrt y. Add the density contributions from both branches:

    fY(y)=ϕ(y)2y+ϕ(−y)2y=e−y/22πy,y>0\begin{aligned}f_Y(y)&=\frac{\phi(\sqrt y)}{2\sqrt y}+\frac{\phi(-\sqrt y)}{2\sqrt y}\\&=\frac{e^{-y/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi y}},\qquad y>0\end{aligned}

    This is a gamma density with shape 1/21/2 and scale 2. ϕ\phi denotes the standard normal density.

  3. Add independent gamma variables with the same scale.

    Their shapes add. One way to see that is through moment-generating functions: independence multiplies them.

    MZ2(s)=(1−2s)−1/2MQ(s)=[MZ2(s)]ν=(1−2s)−ν/2,s<12\begin{aligned}M_{Z^2}(s)&=(1-2s)^{-1/2}\\M_Q(s)&=[M_{Z^2}(s)]^\nu\\&=(1-2s)^{-\nu/2},\quad s<\frac12\end{aligned}
    fQ(q)=qν/2−1e−q/22ν/2Γ(ν/2),q>0f_Q(q)=\frac{q^{\nu/2-1}e^{-q/2}}{2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)},\qquad q>0

    The gamma function generalizes factorials: Γ(a)=∫0∞ua−1e−u du\Gamma(a)=\int_0^\infty u^{a-1}e^{-u}\,du, and Γ(m)=(m−1)!\Gamma(m)=(m-1)! for positive integers.

  4. Calculate the mean and variance.

    E[Z2]=1Var⁡(Z2)=E[Z4]−1=3−1=2\begin{aligned}\mathbb E[Z^2]&=1\\\operatorname{Var}(Z^2)&=\mathbb E[Z^4]-1\\&=3-1=2\end{aligned}
    E[Q]=ν,Var⁡(Q)=2ν\mathbb E[Q]=\nu,\qquad \operatorname{Var}(Q)=2\nu

    Independence lets the variances add. More components shift the distribution right. Relative fluctuations shrink, so the distribution becomes less skewed.

  5. Read probability as area.

    Pr⁡(Q≥t)=∫t∞fQ(q) dq\Pr(Q\geq t)=\int_t^\infty f_Q(q)\,dq

    A density height is not a probability at a point. The shaded area is the chance of obtaining a sum at least as large as the threshold.

Four squared errors

If the standardized errors are 1, −2, 0.5, and 0, the sum is 1+4+0.25+0=5.251+4+0.25+0=5.25. Under four independent standard normals, a sum at least this large occurs with probability about 0.263. For 4 degrees of freedom, the usual upper 5% threshold is about 9.49.

Why is sample variance related to chi-square?

For independent normal measurements, if μ\mu is known, the sum of squared standardized deviations has nn degrees of freedom:

∑i=1n(Xi−μ)2σ2∼χn2\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(X_i-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_n

When you estimate the mean from the data, the residuals must sum to zero. That uses one dimension:

∑i=1n(Xi−Xˉ)=0,(n−1)S2σ2∼χn−12\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X)=0,\qquad \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}

Degrees of freedom count the independent directions left after constraints or fitted parameters. For non-normal populations, this sample-variance chi-square result generally is not exact.

Derive Pearson’s statistic and its degrees of freedom

  1. State the model before looking for a discrepancy.

    H0: p1=⋯=p6=16Ej=Npj=N6\begin{aligned}H_0:&\ p_1=\cdots=p_6=\frac16\\E_j&=Np_j=\frac N6\end{aligned}

    OjO_j is the observed count and EjE_j is its expected count under the null. The total NN is the sum of the six observed counts.

  2. Scale discrepancies to make them comparable.

    Rj=Oj−EjEjQ=∑j=16Rj2=∑j=16(Oj−Ej)2Ej\begin{aligned}R_j&=\frac{O_j-E_j}{\sqrt{E_j}}\\Q&=\sum_{j=1}^{6}R_j^2\\&=\sum_{j=1}^{6}\frac{(O_j-E_j)^2}{E_j}\end{aligned}

    A difference of 10 is much larger relative to ordinary noise when the expected count is 10 than when it is 1,000. The contributions are 10 and 0.1 respectively. Squaring stops positive and negative differences from canceling.

    These Pearson residuals are correlated. For fixed-total multinomial counts, each count’s exact variance is Npj(1−pj)Np_j(1-p_j), not simply EjE_j. We do not pretend the six residuals are six independent standard normals.

  3. Use the constraint to count free directions.

    ∑j=16Oj=∑j=16Ej=N⇓∑j=16(Oj−Ej)=0\begin{gathered}\sum_{j=1}^{6}O_j=\sum_{j=1}^{6}E_j=N\\\Downarrow\\\sum_{j=1}^{6}(O_j-E_j)=0\end{gathered}

    Once five count discrepancies are known, the sixth is determined. In a large sample the standardized residual vector behaves like normal noise in a five-dimensional subspace. Its squared length therefore approaches a chi-square distribution with 5 degrees of freedom.

    Q ≈H0 χ52Q\ \overset{H_0}{\approx}\ \chi^2_5

  4. Ask how often the null produces at least this much discrepancy.

    p-value=Pr⁡H0(Q≥Qobserved)p\text{-value}=\Pr_{H_0}(Q\geq Q_{\mathrm{observed}})

    A small p-value is evidence against this fair-die model. A large one means these data do not provide strong evidence against it; it does not prove fairness. The p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

One face with too many outcomes

For counts 10, 10, 10, 10, 10, 50, the total is 100 and each expected count is 16.67. The five low faces contribute about 2.67 each; the sixth contributes about 66.67. Thus Q=80Q=80, an extremely large statistic compared with ordinary χ52\chi^2_5 variation.

The chi-square reference is asymptotic. This lesson flags expected counts below 5 as a common caution rule. Sparse cases can be checked with a suitable exact or Monte Carlo multinomial test. If model parameters are fitted from these same counts, degrees of freedom may also decrease; the usual formula is categories − 1 − fitted parameters under the relevant regularity and fitting conditions.

See the geometry behind the chi-square approximation

For mm fixed-probability categories, define v=(p1,…,pm)Tv=(\sqrt{p_1},\ldots,\sqrt{p_m})^T, which has length 1, and let RR be the Pearson residual vector.

Cov⁡(R)=Im−vvTvTR=∑j(Oj−Npj)N=0\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Cov}(R)&=I_m-vv^T\\v^TR&=\frac{\sum_j(O_j-Np_j)}{\sqrt N}=0\end{aligned}

Here ImI_m is the identity matrix, not Fisher information. The covariance matrix removes the one direction parallel to vv and preserves the other m−1m-1 orthogonal directions. The multinomial central limit theorem gives asymptotically independent standard normal coordinates in that subspace.

RTR →d ∑j=1m−1Zj2∼χm−12R^TR\ \xrightarrow{d}\ \sum_{j=1}^{m-1}Z_j^2\sim\chi^2_{m-1}

This explains both the chi-square shape and the lost degree of freedom without assuming independence of the original residuals.

Derive all three tests for the coin

  1. Score: is the slope at the claim unusually large?

    S=U(p0)2In(p0)=(k−np0)2np0(1−p0)S=\frac{U(p_0)^2}{I_n(p_0)}=\frac{(k-np_0)^2}{np_0(1-p_0)}

    The score’s null variance is information, so dividing by its standard deviation standardizes it. Squaring detects disagreement in either direction. The score test only needs the null model.

    U(p0)In(p0)→dN(0,1)⟹S→dχ12\frac{U(p_0)}{\sqrt{I_n(p_0)}}\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S\xrightarrow{d}\chi^2_1

  2. Wald: how far is the estimate from the claim?

    W=(p^−p0)2Var⁡^(p^)=n(p^−p0)2p^(1−p^)W=\frac{(\hat p-p_0)^2}{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat p)}=\frac{n(\hat p-p_0)^2}{\hat p(1-\hat p)}

    This uses the estimated standard error at p^=k/n\hat p=k/n, whereas score uses the null standard error at p0p_0. That difference matters in small samples and near boundaries.

  3. Likelihood ratio: how much fit does the claim cost?

    G2=2[ℓ(p^)−ℓ(p0)]G^2=2[\ell(\hat p)-\ell(p_0)]
    G2=2[klog⁡p^p0+(n−k)log⁡1−p^1−p0]G^2=2\left[k\log\frac{\hat p}{p_0}+(n-k)\log\frac{1-\hat p}{1-p_0}\right]

    The unconstrained model always fits at least as well, so this statistic is nonnegative. Under a regular interior null and large samples, Wilks’ theorem gives the same χ12\chi^2_1 reference: one free parameter was constrained.

  4. Why do these three agree locally?

    Near the fitted peak, approximate the log likelihood by a parabola:

    ℓ(p0)≈ℓ(p^)−12Jn(p^)(p0−p^)2\ell(p_0)\approx\ell(\hat p)-\frac12J_n(\hat p)(p_0-\hat p)^2
    U(p0)≈−Jn(p^)(p0−p^)In(p0)≈In(p^)=Jn(p^)\begin{aligned}U(p_0)&\approx-J_n(\hat p)(p_0-\hat p)\\I_n(p_0)&\approx I_n(\hat p)=J_n(\hat p)\end{aligned}
    S≈W≈G2≈In(p^)(p^−p0)2S\approx W\approx G^2\approx I_n(\hat p)(\hat p-p_0)^2

    The three methods measure the same local discrepancy as slope, horizontal distance, or vertical loss. Their large-sample equivalence concerns the null and nearby alternatives; it is not a promise that their numerical values agree for every distant alternative.

100 tosses, 60 heads, claimed p₀ = 0.50

The score statistic is 4.000, Wald is 4.167, and likelihood ratio is about 4.027. Their approximate p-values are about 0.0455, 0.0412, and 0.0448. Try the small-sample preset to see the approximation and methods separate.

Show that the score test equals a two-category Pearson test

Treat heads and tails as the two categories. Their discrepancies are equal in magnitude with opposite signs:

Q=(k−np0)2np0+[(n−k)−n(1−p0)]2n(1−p0)Q=\frac{(k-np_0)^2}{np_0}+\frac{[(n-k)-n(1-p_0)]^2}{n(1-p_0)}
Q=(k−np0)2[1np0+1n(1−p0)]=(k−np0)2np0(1−p0)=S\begin{aligned}Q&=(k-np_0)^2\left[\frac1{np_0}+\frac1{n(1-p_0)}\right]\\&=\frac{(k-np_0)^2}{np_0(1-p_0)}\\&=S\end{aligned}

Two categories minus one fixed-total constraint leaves one degree of freedom. The score, information, and Pearson viewpoints are directly connected.

What does the exact binomial comparison mean?

The lesson also calculates a finite-sample p-value using the score statistic’s own ordering. Sum probabilities of all counts whose score statistic is at least the observed one:

pexact,S=∑j:S(j)≥S(k)(nj)p0j(1−p0)n−jp_{\mathrm{exact},S}=\sum_{j:S(j)\geq S(k)}\binom njp_0^j(1-p_0)^{n-j}

This calculation is exact under the binomial null and exposes discreteness that a continuous chi-square approximation misses. It is a two-sided score-ordered p-value; other definitions of a two-sided exact binomial test, such as probability ordering, can produce different values.

The simple Wald formula is undefined for all heads or all tails, so the sliders keep both outcomes present. Small expected counts and parameters close to boundaries can make chi-square approximations unreliable. The score-tail sum remains finite-sample valid.
